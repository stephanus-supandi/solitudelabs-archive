## 2. Shared research theme: spatial memory and urban change

The two articles can be read together through three distinct layers:

1. **Observed / mapped space** — streets, signs, buildings, landmarks, businesses, and map records that can be revisited or checked.
2. **Remembered space** — names, routes, former tenants, and personal recollections that help generate search questions.
3. **Documented space** — dated photographs, official records, directories, maps, corporate documents, and other sources that can corroborate or contradict a recollection.

These layers must not be collapsed into one another. A remembered place is a useful lead, not proof. A directory entry is evidence that a listing existed, not proof that the business operates there today. A landmark can orient a route, but it does not independently establish the history of nearby properties.

## 3. Methodological principle

> Memory is not proof that a place existed at a particular address. Memory can, however, be a starting point for investigation and documentation.

Suggested workflow:

1. Record the recollection in its original, qualified form.
2. Identify candidate place names, date ranges, and possible landmarks.
3. Search maps, dated imagery, directories, official records, and corporate documents.
4. Compare sources while preserving disagreements.
5. Separate direct observation, reported information, inference, and unknowns.
6. State what evidence would be needed to resolve each open question.

This workflow is consistent with the SolitudeLabs method: **Observe. Investigate. Document. Verify.**

## 5. Editorial interpretation: what remains and what disappears

A possible comparative theme is the contrast between a street or landmark that remains visible and a commercial place remembered after its brand or tenant has changed. This can be explored as an essay about how people navigate cities through both physical references and memory.

However, the archive should not claim that the city deliberately erased a place, that a remembered business occupied a specific unit, or that a statue is a permanent anchor unless evidence supports those claims.

The strongest version of the argument is modest: **urban memory can outlast a tenant, a sign, or a familiar commercial name, while documentary evidence may be incomplete.** The task is to record the memory, test it where possible, and preserve the unresolved remainder.
